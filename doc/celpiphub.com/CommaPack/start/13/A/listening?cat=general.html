WOMAN: Sure. We offer one class each week on Wednesdays, usually with a special theme for the month. For example, this is June and the theme is barbecuing.

MAN: My wife said she wants me to do some of the summer cooking. I can barbecue, so your classes might be very helpful. But wait, now that I think of it, I actually wanted to ask about the Indian curry class advertised in your flyer. We love curry. When is it scheduled?

Instructions:

You will hear a presentation about children and TV.

Practice Test A - Listening Part 6: Listening for Viewpoints

Listen to the following report. You will hear the report only once. It is about 3 minutes long.

Playing...
Click "NEXT" to continue.
Show Transcript

Many of us will remember, as children, waking up early Saturday morning, trekking downstairs while our parents slept and flipping on our favorite Saturday morning cartoons. A weekend ritual that entertained children and allowed weary parents a few extra hours of sleep, Saturday morning cartoons seemed like a win-win situation. Indeed, despite their primary purpose being a means to advertise products to a young and captive audience for several hours a week, most parents likely appreciated the brief reprieve that they provided.

However, Natalie Kingsley, spokesperson for the parent advocacy group, “Turn it off,” contends that children today watch far more than just a few hours of TV a week--the current average being closer to two hours daily. With most Canadian homes now owning multiple televisions, in addition to other electronic devices, such as computers, tablets, or cellphones, children now have access to round the clock TV programming and games. Kingsley believes the excessive time spent in these sedentary behaviors can be blamed for the increasing prevalence of disorders such as obesity and Attention Deficit Disorder seen in children today. She also aptly points out the disturbing trend towards infant and toddler specific television programming, such as LaLa TV, which, despite warnings from the majority of pediatricians that children under two should never watch TV, are becoming increasingly popular.

Yet, for Maya Pang, a working mother of three, LaLa TV has been a lifesaver. While Pang understands the importance of not letting her 10-month old watch TV all day, she is grateful for the hour of quiet provided when her child watches his favorite TV show, a rare moment of calm that allows her to get supper prepared. Being a parent herself, Kingsley understands Pang’s sentiment, however, she asserts that, although this one hour may seem harmless, before the age of three, children learn better from the real world. They may be fascinated and entertained by the images they see on a screen, but they are unable to make sense of them. Time spent watching TV takes away from time that should be spent exploring the real world, a fact which can have adverse effects on learning, in particular, language development. While Pang is aware of these concerns, she believes parents can drive themselves nuts trying to be perfect. Letting her son watch LaLa TV each day gives her the time to prepare a healthy, home-cooked dinner for her family. Before LaLa TV, a fussy infant combined with Pang’s busy schedule, meant dinners often consisted of take-out or frozen meals. For Pang, although not a perfect solution, it is one that works for her family.
