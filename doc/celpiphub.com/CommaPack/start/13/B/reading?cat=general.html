Of course, Anne herself is a fictional character in the novels of Lucy Maud Montgomery. Shopkeepers market items based on the novels, even straw hats with red braids so that young girls can look like Anne. She is also celebrated in a permanently running live musical in the capital city of Charlottetown.

B. Anne and her story have attracted Canadian fans since 1908. The dated language and Anne’s enthusiastic monologues of the original text are challenging to read, but once you meet Anne Shirley, rescued from an orphanage by Matthew and Marilla Cuthbert, you are hooked into her life. Anne’s story is based on Montgomery’s own life. Born in a PEI village, Montgomery was an orphan by age seven and raised by her strict, Cuthbert-like grandparents in Cavendish. Montgomery had a lonely childhood and depended on imaginary friends, just like Anne did. Like Anne, Montgomery was lively, intelligent, and flirtatious, and, like Anne, she supported herself as a teacher.

C. Montgomery wrote six novels about Anne, portraying her life from age 11 to 40. The first book, Anne of Green Gables, appeals to women of all ages. Many readers have tried to analyze this appeal, which boils down to this: Anne shows us the key to essential happiness. She represents all women, especially during the formative years, wanting to be smart, funny, honest, and optimistic. Anne agonized over her red hair, frequently a stigma that invites teasing. All women may not worry about red hair, but all worry about their faults and their looks, especially at Anne’s age in the first book.
