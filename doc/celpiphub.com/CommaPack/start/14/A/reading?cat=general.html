Thanks to Caribana, about $400 million a year flows into Ontario, about half of which goes to the government in tax revenues.

B. Caribana—the name is intended to suggest both the Caribbean and Canada—started in 1967 as the Caribbean community’s contribution to Canada’s centennial celebrations. It celebrates not only Canada’s centennial, but also Caribbean culture and the African emancipation, when the enslavement of African people in Canada was declared illegal on August 1, 1834. However, since the 1980s, Caribana has included elaborate and impressive contributions from cultural groups in Central America, South America, and Africa, as well.

C. The festival celebrates music with indigenous songs and instrumental music including calypso, soca, reggae, and salsa. Practice Test A - Reading Part 4: Reading for Viewpoints

Time remaining: 13 minutes

Read the following article from a website.

Ever heard of Firmicutes, Bacteroidetes, or Verrucomicrobia? While these names might sound like an alien species from a thriller movie, they are, in fact, microscopic residents within our very own bodies. Indeed, they’re just a few of the many microorganisms which make up the human microbiome, a term which refers to all of the microorganisms within the human body. It turns out that a healthy human body is teeming with a wide variety of microorganisms, including bacteria, fungi, and even viruses, with many human ailments linked to a reduction in this diversity.

According to researcher Ellie Kim, her investigations have found correlations between human microbiomes and a diverse array of diseases. “The role that these tiny creatures appear to have on health is profound and will open doors to new kinds of medical treatments,” Kim enthuses. She envisions that correcting microbiome imbalances may soon replace the practice of wiping out entire colonies of microorganisms with antibiotics. Indeed, with antibiotic-resistant bacteria on the rise, our current methods of treating bacterial illnesses are becoming worryingly obsolete.

Dietician Carlos Gomez is unconvinced. He contends that it isn’t clear whether the microbiome changes associated with various diseases are the cause of, or simply the secondary effect, of disease. “Studies have found both a decrease in gut diversity and a rise in gastrointestinal illnesses when travellers eat new dishes,” says Gomez. He acknowledges that gut microorganisms typically undergo changes when digesting unfamiliar foods. Gomez emphasizes, “It’s uncertain whether supplementing yourself with a better balance of microorganisms, without an accompanying avoidance of these foods, would impact overall gut health.”

Magali Beauchamp, however, is willing to take her chances. Although stomach troubles aren’t a concern for her, Beauchamp struggled with eczema—a skin condition—for years. She tried an array of the usual topical treatments, but to no avail. Under her doctor’s supervision, she resorted to probiotics, which are live microorganisms which can be consumed to balance the body’s microbiome. Within months, she found relief. A now healthy Beauchamp markets probiotic foods to stores across Canada, believing everyone can benefit from a few extra critters.
