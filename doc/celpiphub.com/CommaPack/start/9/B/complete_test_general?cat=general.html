Area resident Beverley Camaro has built a house of straw.

Camaro moved into the area last year, and had been living in a trailer on a small plot of land in the countryside on which she intended to build a small house. Concerned about growing heating costs and increasingly colder winter temperatures, she spent a month researching heating and housing construction. Practice Test B - Listening Part 6: Listening for Viewpoints

Listen to the following report. You will hear the report only once. It is about 3 minutes long.

Playing...
Click "NEXT" to continue.
Show Transcript

Today on Chat Radio: Should online social networking sites, such as Facebook or Twitter, be used in schools? Social networking sites, where users share messages, pictures, or videos with others, are an increasingly popular form of communication for both adults and children. In an effort to keep education relevant, many are asking whether such sites should, perhaps, be incorporated into school curricula.

For Sheena Bradley, CEO of LAB-Tech Industries, this question is easily answered. She argues that education is meant to prepare students for their future which will likely involve working with others via online platforms. For example, LAB-Tech uses an in-house social networking site called ThinkTank to encourage employee collaboration and the development of new ideas, something that has led to a substantial increase in productivity and innovation among employees. For Bradley, we are at the dawn of a new work culture where the innovative group is valued. Accordingly, she sees online social networking as a popular medium for acquiring the skills that today’s employers want: technological knowledge and the ability to work with others.

James Spadoni, a teacher at Crestview Secondary, agrees. However, while Spadoni is clear that online social networking should be incorporated into the curriculum, how to achieve this is less clear for him. Spadoni, being someone nearing sixty, did not grow up using computers. He thus struggles to learn how new technologies can be used to attain a pedagogical goal. Despite this challenge, Spadoni has found online social networking to be quite successful in the classroom. As part of last year’s final class project, Spadoni’s class used Twitter to share ideas and organize workflow. This proved particularly effective for shyer students who tended to feel more comfortable sharing ideas in an online environment.

Mandy Rainer, however, is not convinced. She wants her children to be learning at school, not playing around on the internet. She sees no need to include online social networking as part of the school curriculum, insisting that students already get enough exposure outside of school. For Rainer, who already struggles to get her kids off Facebook, children need less socializing, not more. Children need to learn to concentrate, to focus on a task, and to read without distraction. It’s Rainer’s opinion that social media and teamwork certainly won’t help students write their own research essays. To prepare for university and many professional careers, they must be able to work independently and rely on themselves.

Many parent groups agree with Rainer and would like to see social media and cellphones banned in the classroom. What do our listeners think? Call us and share your opinion.
