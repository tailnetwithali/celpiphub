Part 1: Listening to Problem Solving

You are about to hear a conversation between a man and a woman at a community garden. The man recently joined the community garden; the woman is a council member and gardener.


Section 1:

MAN: Hello! I'm Mark.

WOMAN: Hello! I'm Teresa. You’re new here, yes? I've been away for a few months, which would explain why we haven't met yet. How's your garden coming along?

MAN: Yes, I'm relatively new. And I’m afraid I’m having some troubles already.

WOMAN: Oh no! What’s going on?

MAN: Someone’s been stealing my tomatoes!

WOMAN: Oh no! That’s so sad. Unfortunately, it’s pretty common in a community garden. Sorry to hear that though.

MAN: Any idea who's stealing them?

WOMAN: I have no idea. It’s hard to believe it might be a fellow garden member, but you never know! Or sometimes kids like to come through and have a snack. I’ve caught them eating my raspberries a few times.

MAN: Do you think I should put a sign up?

WOMAN: There’s no harm in trying, but to be honest, I doubt a sign will stop anyone.


Now answer questions 1-2.

1- What is the man unhappy about?
2- What is the woman’s reaction to the man’s news?

Section 2:

MAN: Hello again!

WOMAN: Hi there! How’s the garden?

MAN: Not so good. I put up a sign, but more tomatoes are gone today. I haven’t been able to eat a single ripe tomato! Only the green ones are left.

WOMAN: Well, you could pick the green ones and make a green tomato salsa!

MAN: I'm not much of a salsa fan. Do you have any other suggestions?

WOMAN: This is a sneaky one, but you could sprinkle some baking flour on your green tomatoes.

MAN: Baking flour? What good would that do?

WOMAN: People will think it’s insecticide or pesticide or something, so they won't want your tomatoes!

MAN: I would never have thought of that! Thanks!


Now answer questions 3-5.

3- Why does the woman mention green tomatoes?
4- Why does the woman suggest sprinkling flour on the tomatoes?
5- What is the man likely to do next?

Section 3:

WOMAN: Hey, how are the tomatoes?

MAN: There’s a few red ones! The flour trick seems to be working. But I had a new thought. Maybe rabbits are eating my tomatoes.

WOMAN: Hmm. When I think of rabbits in gardens, I think of them eating lettuce and green leafy stuff, but maybe they like tomatoes too.

MAN: Anyway, I decided to put a wire fence around my tomato section, you know, out of that silver chicken wire netting.

WOMAN: Oh, I'm afraid we have a policy against that sort of thing. There was a vote last year and most people said they didn't want—well, they didn’t think wire fencing was attractive.

MAN: Oh, really? That's too bad.

WOMAN: How about a wooden fence instead?

MAN: That would be okay. Sure, I'll do that then.

6- What does the man think is responsible for his problem?

WOMAN: No problem. But if you get the job, you owe me lunch!

WOMAN: Welcome to Aquaworld! How can I help you?

MAN: Hi! I'm interested in purchasing a fish aquarium. I'm an animal lover and I've had all different kinds of pets, but never fish. I’ve always wanted to try owning my own aquarium.

WOMAN: Well, you've come to the right place! I can certainly help you get started. Now, were you considering a freshwater aquarium, or a saltwater one?

MAN: I've always wanted a saltwater aquarium. I love tropical fish and coral reefs. But I've heard that freshwater tanks are easier for beginners to maintain.

WOMAN: That is generally true. However, it's more the size of your tank setup that will determine how hard it is to take care of.

MAN: Right, I see. A big tank is more difficult to take care of than a little one, of course.

WOMAN: Actually, that's a very common misconception! It sounds strange, but the larger the tank, the easier it is to take care of. This is because a larger volume of water helps to maintain temperature changes and the quality of the water.

MAN: Wow, I didn't know that! Do you think I should get saltwater then?

WOMAN: If you think this is going to be a serious hobby for you, I would get the aquarium you will enjoy most. It sounds like you want a saltwater tank. Now, to be honest with you, saltwater aquariums generally cost more than freshwater ones, because they tend to require additional equipment. If you want living corals, they usually need special lighting.

MAN: I think I will get saltwater. And yes, I'd like corals. They're so colorful! They're actually the main reason I'd prefer saltwater to freshwater. So, in addition to a tank, fish, and corals, what are the basics that I need to buy?

WOMAN: You will need a good filtration system, which continuously cleans the water. You will also need sand for the tank floor, and marine salt to add to the water. Then you need lighting, and a water test kit, and food for the fish of course.

MAN: Okay, sounds good! Would you be able to show me where I could get all these things?

WOMAN: Absolutely. Follow me and we can get started!

Part 4: Listening to a News Item

You will hear a news item about a surprise at a local high school.

The principal at a local Winnipeg high school couldn’t believe his eyes when he discovered a valuable artifact at work.

Edward O’Reilly has been working at Winnipeg Secondary School for over 15 years, first as an art teacher, and later as its principal. While moving some office furniture last week, O’Reilly discovered a large, beautiful painting hidden behind an old filing cabinet, once used by his predecessor to store school records. The original painting, lushly coloured with bright watercolours, was painted by the expert hand of legendary painter Alejandra Leland. It was later appraised by local art experts to be valued at approximately ten thousand dollars.

Speaking with reporters at a press conference, O’Reilly recalled why he was rearranging his office in the first place. He said that the school janitor had asked him to move the filing cabinet so that he could reach the ceiling vent and do a quick repair. O’Reilly was extremely surprised to find the treasure since he does spring cleaning of his office each year. However, he never thought to look behind the old filing cabinet. The discovery is opportune as now the school has resources to add music to their curriculum.


Part 5: Listening to a Discussion

You will watch a discussion among three people, two men and one woman, at a gas station office. They are dealing with an issue at their workplace.

Daniel: Hey Jerry, the washrooms need to be cleaned. Do you mind doing it before you take your lunchbreak?

Jerry: Yeah, sure thing . . . just let me finish sending this text message. It hasn’t been that busy, so I’m sure they’re not that bad.

Louise: Don’t worry, Daniel. I did them a couple hours ago, and only a few customers have used them since then. Besides, Jerry’s making plans with his girlfriend. You don’t want to mess up his weekend, do you?

Daniel: Sometimes I just don’t understand your generation . . . all your texting and cellphones; you spend so much time with your faces buried in a screen, that you don’t even talk to the people standing right in front of you! When I was a teenager, I would call my girlfriend up, or talk to her face to face! This new age of technology is so impersonal!

Louise: You and my mom would get along great! But times change. We save so much time by texting. We can get things done without wasting time chatting on the phone—it’s why our generation is great at multi-tasking!

Jerry: Totally. With texting I can make plans with my girlfriend and help customers at the same time. You cannot do that while you’re talking on the phone.

Daniel: Well, let’s not forget I didn’t hire you to socialize, I hired you to work. When your face is buried in your phone, you can’t even see the customers! I know you guys aren’t planning to stay here forever, you don’t care about promotions, but if you want to get a decent letter of recommendation from me when you decide to move on, you’ll put your phones away.

Jerry: Woah. Sorry Daniel . . . I didn’t realize it bothered you so much. Listen, I really need this reference for my university application. If I don’t get into university, I don’t stand much chance of getting my dream job. I’ll save the texting for my breaks.

Louise: I don’t want to mess up my reference either, but I don’t get the problem. We live in a digital world that keeps moving and changing. If you don’t stay connected, it’s hard to get caught up. I’m not just sending silly texts . . . I’m doing my banking, sending emails, researching information. I really don’t see how it interferes with getting my work done . . . but if you insist, I’ll put my phone away.

Daniel: Thanks, you two! I may be old fashioned, but so are most of my customers. And I know that they don’t appreciate being served by somebody whose focus is more on their phone than on them.


Part 6: Listening for Viewpoints

You will hear a presentation about a language learning approach.

Calgary-based English as a Second Language teacher Mark Dugard has pledged to give up his intimate relationship with his ESL textbooks. Dugard has just embarked on an experiment with a radically outside-the-box language teaching approach called Lexilearn English Language Teaching. Lexilearn-style lessons are based on spontaneous, free-flowing, and undirected student conversations. Publishers’ textbooks and workbooks are shunned, as are syllabi, course outlines, and even lesson plans. Instead, the learning objectives are inductively derived from whatever language naturally emerges from the students. Lexilearn isn’t so much a teaching method as an anti-method.

One Lexilearn devotee is Madison Chang, who teaches conversational English at Toronto’s Interglobe Learning Centre. She adopted the Lexilearn approach two years ago and hasn’t looked back. In the past, she says, she would slavishly follow the dialogues, stories, and grammar in the textbook, but now she and her students collaboratively create these texts as they emerge from natural conversations. Chang says students are much more engaged with and take greater pride in texts they’ve authored and grammar they’ve produced themselves. With Lexilearn, Chang feels more attuned to her students, listening intently to everything they say so that she can tease out narratives and extrapolate relevant grammatical structures. Chang says Lexilearn has made her a more creative and engaged teacher; it forces her to think on her feet.

But Regina College principal Evan Kasowitz derides the Lexilearn approach as nothing more than a dignified term for “winging it.” Kasowitz, who trains the school’s ESL teachers, notes that the few-and-far-between teachers who succeed with the approach tend to be unusually gifted at making up activities as they go along, even when preparation was obviously needed. Kasowitz also charges that Lexilearn is elitist, because its practitioners must be highly fluent native speakers of English—a rarity among ESL teachers worldwide. And even with an impeccable Lexilearn teacher, the approach still doesn’t work with many students: those wanting test preparation, those from cultures where textbooks are seen as giving credibility to the course, and, finally, Kasowitz says with an ominous tone, teenagers. Don’t ever try Lexilearn in a large classroom of adolescents, Kasowitz warns. It will fail beyond your worst nightmare.

Happily for Mark Dugard back in Calgary, his classroom is full of adults. Whether or not Lexilearn becomes a permanent fixture in his classroom, Dugard says the first 15 minutes of his class will always be what he calls “not true Lexilearn, but certainly Lexilearn-esque.” Starting every class with undirected, voluntary conversation, Dugard says, puts the students at ease and prepares them to learn. But that’s not Lexilearn, insists Dugard; it’s just a good warmup.

Part 6: Listening for Viewpoints

You will hear a presentation about social responsibility.

The sole responsibility of a corporation, according to economist Milton Friedman, is to make money. A corporation exists to serve its shareholders—that is, any entity which owns any part of the company—whose goal is to increase the value of their investment in that company. As long as the decisions made by a company’s executives further this aim, a corporation is fulfilling its duty.

Profits aside, if a company’s actions harm its employees, customers, the community, or the environment, surely this must be problematic. In recent decades, more people have realized that these affected groups, known as stakeholders, deserve consideration in corporate decision making. In contrast to Friedman’s shareholder view, the stakeholder view contends that corporations have an obligation to integrate social and environmental concerns into their business model. For example, corporations should contribute to community development, pay respectable wages, or donate to worthy causes.

For Milton Friedman, however, addressing social issues is the responsibility of the government, not corporations. Asking companies to spend revenue on social welfare is akin to imposing taxation on shareholders. Friedman makes it clear that corporations are not public servants and so are not responsible to the public. What’s more, a corporation, as an artificial construct, does not have moral or social responsibilities in the same sense as people. For Friedman, the sole social responsibility of a corporation is to maximize profits while obeying the law.

Ethicist Margaret White says Friedman is right in one sense. While it would undoubtedly be kind for a rich company to give money to a community struggling economically, we can no more require a company to hand out donations than we can require a person to give money to someone on the street who asks for change. Charity should not be mandated.

White points out that in many cases, however, corporations are inextricably linked to social or environmental problems. Many companies, for example, find legal loopholes to irresponsibly dispose of their waste. Though they manage to skirt the law, companies, just like people, should be held morally responsible for the consequences of their actions.

Interestingly, whether companies agree or disagree with White, it’s now in their interest to adopt a socially responsible position. With the rise of ethics-influenced consumerism, shoppers are demanding that products be produced with the concerns of labourers, animals, and the environment in mind. Sadly, as a by-product, this has also given rise to a form of “greenwashing” in advertising, where companies market themselves with an environmentally friendly image for the sole interest of increasing sales.
