Listening Part 6: Listening for Viewpoints.

You will hear a presentation about cycling in the city.

The addition of bike lanes in densely populated urban areas throughout major Canadian cities is on the rise, creating tension between cyclists and car drivers.

Shannon Tulu, an avid biker and campaigner for city cycling in Toronto, says that bike lanes have been absent for too long in city planning. She believes it is the long wait that has created a culture of car drivers that are now irrationally annoyed at having to accommodate for different modes of transport. The municipal government, meanwhile, is hesitant to support cyclists over drivers. And yet, had major bike lanes been around twenty years ago, Tulu suggests, the bike culture would have grown since then, and no one would be batting an eye now. She points to cities such as Amsterdam, where 40% of commuting is done by bike and the infrastructure has been in place for decades.

For Tulu, the main need for bike lanes is safety. Having been in two serious bike accidents in which no bike lane was available, Tulu understands the need for a designated cyclist roadway. Tulu says statistics show that less bike accidents occur when cyclists have their own lane.

Fred Morris is the chair of the Provincial Mountain Biking Association. His passion for cycling does not, however, place him in the same camp as Tulu. On the contrary, Morris counters that while recreational bicycling is wonderful, bikes have no place in city traffic. Five years ago, he was in an accident in which he was cycling in a designated lane. Morris argues bike lanes create the illusion of safety because cyclists assume drivers are looking out for them. He says bike lanes actually increase the chance of car drivers hitting cyclists when they accidentally turn in front of them. Morris criticized cyclists for being aggressive. They don’t want to lose their momentum and come to a full stop at intersections and so they take chances to maintain speed.

Morris agrees with Tulu that if more city bike lanes had been built in Toronto decades ago, things would be much different. More bike lanes then would have meant a much stronger bike culture now, and car drivers would be more accepting and aware. But Morris says it’s much too late to try and add bike lanes into already dense, populated areas. Doing so, he says, will just cause more aggression on both sides.

Tulu disagrees. In a time when cities are trying to reduce fossil fuel emissions and encourage environmentally friendly ways of living, she argues, developing a generation of bicycle commuters is crucial to the health of our cities and our planet.

Part 4: Listening to a News Item

You will hear a news item about a community development issue.

Despite financial troubles experienced by bike-sharing programs in Montreal and Toronto, Ottawa is planning to introduce a similar program. The program would enable people to rent a bike from a number of self-serve stations for short-term use. The city plans to subsidize the program with a $60,000 grant, which would fund the construction of ten solar-powered park-and-pay stations across the city. The grant would also fund the purchase of the bicycles themselves, which feature three speeds, comfortable seats, reflectors and lights, a luggage carrier, and a lock. Operating costs are expected to be covered by user fees.

It is hoped that the program will foster healthy transit alternatives by reducing the use of automobiles and their consequent pollution and parking problems. Opponents of the program aren’t convinced that user fees will be sufficient to cover operating costs: either they’ll be too high to afford or too low to cover expenses. At a recent city council meeting, one councillor argued that people should have to buy their own bikes, and the existing public transit system could use the budget to expand and improve its efficiency.

Part 6: Listening for Viewpoints

You will hear a presentation about a wildlife issue.

Sable Island, a remote island located 300 km southeast of Halifax, has long been romanticized in many Canadians’ minds as a place where wild horses run free. Brought to the Atlantic provinces by British and French colonists in the eighteenth century, these draft horses once served as farm animals, but were eventually abandoned on the island. Today, Parks Canada released a report suggesting that these famous horses may be in danger of extinction.

Parks Canada spokeswoman, Sheila McNeil, says that according to a recent study commissioned by the department of Species at Risk, there are a variety of factors that may lead to the horses’ extinction in the future. One concern is inbreeding, as these horses have become more susceptible to disease. Another concern is the extreme coastal weather, because the conditions can produce heavy snowfalls which will bury the horses' food source. Each of these issues are more concerning because the horses’ population is so low. If anything were to cause their numbers to decrease further, they’re that much closer to extinction. McNeil reports that Parks Canada is reviewing the data and, as it stands, Parks Canada feels the horses should remain on the island.

Contrary to McNeil, Biologist Malcolm Watson argues that the horse population is higher than ever, and that these horses are ecologically intrusive to the island’s natural species. Watson believes they should be removed from the island. He says that the horses threaten plant and animal species by stomping on nests and destroying vegetation as they race around the island. He claims that because Sable Island is now a national park, the legislation requires it be kept pristine, and inhabited only by indigenous species. Therefore, since the horses are not native to Sable Island, Parks Canada has a duty to remove them in order to protect the fragile ecosystem. Moreover, he asserts that the horses are living in appalling conditions, and should be relocated and better cared for.

Nova Scotia resident, Janet Lowe, has heard this argument before and is not too worried. She remembers back in the 1960s when similar concerns were voiced and, at that time, the government ordered the horses be removed from the island. However, due to public outcry, the Prime Minister dropped the removal order and even put laws in place making it illegal for humans to interfere with the horse population. Lowe finds Watson’s argument hypocritical. She says that throughout history, humans have been the most invasive species on the planet, altering and destroying ecosystems for centuries. She thinks that the horses are a far less invasive species, and that the laws enacted in the 60s should be respected.
