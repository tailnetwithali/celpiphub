Voice8:

WOMAN: The Saskatchewan provincial government alongside the University of Saskatchewan have come together to establish 500,000,000 Australian dollars 10 megawatts research nuclear reactor to produce medical isotopes.

Medical isotopes are used in diagnostic procedures for cancer, heart disease, and other medical conditions. When radioactive isotopes are injected into the body, radiologists can view higher radiation via medical imaging, enabling them to make a more accurate diagnosis.

According to Brad Wall, the premier of Saskatchewan, back in 1949, cobalt ’60 treatment for cancer was tried for the first time here in the province where it was used to save a woman who was battling cervical cancer.

Wall suggests that Saskatchewan can again take the lead in terms of nuclear medicine. He asserts that governments should be involved in pure research to deal with certain circumstances as they present themselves.

According to Richard resource at the University of Saskatchewan, the university has faculty who are interested in this resource and that cancer research represents an issue of national importance.

Such research could help the country and this project could be built on the university’s research strength. The proposed research will be conducted at the Canadian light source synchrotron on campus using a research reactor which is intended to enhance the research.

While there has been some criticism, Peter Prebble, director of energy and water policy for the environmental society weighs in. He states that in the case of a power reactor, there are much better alternatives.

However, he states that since this is a medical isotopes research reactor, this is a case where the benefits outweigh the risks. Environmentalist Pauline Collins takes a different view.

She cites that there are several research reactors throughout the world. The reactor at the university, while it might be helpful, could pose more harm than good. According to her the reactor is already 30 years old and is nearing the end of its life where it can operate safely.

She further states that while cancer research is highly important, the immediate safety of residents near the facility is most precedent. The reason for the use of the research reactor at the university is the shutdown of the reactor at Chalk River, Ontario, which occurred last week.

The shutdown was ordered by the Atomic Energy of Canada Limited due to a leak of heavy water and will not reopen for another five years. If the research reactor at University of Saskatchewan is not utilized then Canadian cancer research will be set back for half a decade or more, doctor Thomas Prescott, senior oncologist at Saskatoon General Hospital, notes.

He asserts that this would be a devastating blow to the thousands of Canadians suffering from a multitude of cancers.
