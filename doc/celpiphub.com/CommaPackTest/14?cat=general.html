MAN: It’s nice to meet you. I’d like to ask you about protecting our private information so that it’s not used by corporations.

WOMAN: I’m happy to be here. As a consumer rights advocate, I’ve been following this issue for years. From what I can tell, this situation is becoming more and more prevalent as we are becoming more and more interconnected.

MAN: You mean with online banking and shopping?

WOMAN: Yes. That is exactly what I mean. However, the problem which is generally not known is that these companies are sharing our information with others.

MAN: How are they doing this?

WOMAN: Currently, the problem’s chief cost stems from what is called an updating service.

MAN: Can you describe it, please?

WOMAN: Yes. This service is used by some credit and debit card companies. What it does is that it allows new account numbers and expiry dates to be shared with merchants with whom the customers have dealt with in the past.

MAN: But this would only work with customers who already have existing accounts. Right?

WOMAN: Yes and no. For instance, let’s say your debit card will expire on a certain date. Well, the number will remain the same when you get a new card. But what will happen is that any business, particularly an online business, will be forwarded the new expiry date.

MAN: Well, wouldn’t this be a convenience?

WOMAN: Yes. It would be. But it also sets a situation for that information where an expiry dates and account numbers can be misused.

MAN: Well, no one wants that to happen. So what can be done about it?

WOMAN: Part of the problem is how the banks operate. They do disclose this information, but it’s buried deep in the fine print when they automatically assume their customers agree to this. What you can do is to check with your bank and then see whether you can opt out. If not, then you might want to consider closing your accounts and reopen in some other bank. Make sure though that you can opt out of the service with any new account you open.

Voice7:

MAN: Currently, in British Columbia, public hearings are being held regarding the impacts of a proposed ban on oil tankers in British Columbia seas. On the top of these are competing arguments from first nations who are debating the ban.

The proposal is the Oil Tanker Moratorium Act, which would prohibit oil tankers carrying in excess of 12,500 tons of oil from either stopping or unloading at different ports on the coast. The bill has already been passed in the House of Commons and is now being debated in the Senate.

While the ban has supporters and detractors, what is interesting is that on both sides of the fence are various indigenous groups voicing their opinions regarding the ban. It’s interesting because usually, first nation groups tend to support endeavors that protect the environment.

Take for instance, chief Ronald Black, the leader of the Gitkala Nation, who favors the ban and cites the dangers to the environment that could be caused if it were not in place. He, along with others among his people, believe that it is a duty to keep their territory pristine.

However, this view is not shared by the Laquolams band, which resides near Prince Rupert. According to tribal leaders, they oppose the band because it will curtail any future economic benefits they might enjoy.

According to their leader, Joseph Hegan, his community is facing a lot of economic difficulties, and banning oil tankers in the region could stifle future development of his people.

One MP from Northern British Columbia, Thomas Carlisle, has been a staunch supporter of the ban who cites the need to protect the coastline of British Columbia’s Northern Coast. He notes that this is the position most of his constituents hold, including those belonging to First Nations.

He states that he has won a number of elections because of his stance. Essentially, Carlisle’s support of the ban is to open the field to begin green energy projects as well as improve fish stocks off the coast.

However, according to senator Todd Littlebeck, who is the chairman of the senate committee that is considering the ban, he is opposed to it. He states that oil is the lifeblood of the economy and that it is important to get oil into and out of the ports.

He does acknowledge the danger of spills, but states that overall, spills are rare incidents. He notes that it’s important to ensure that the coastal environment is protected, but it’s equally important to ensure that the local economies are sustainable. Chief Black cites that the ban is not a total ban.

The limitations opposed by it will help protect the environment because it only applies to tankers carrying over 12,500 tons of oil. He states that for this reason alone, there should be a negligible economic impact on the region.

However, Hegan counters this by stating that because of the small amount of oil that is allowed by the ban, there will be an increase in the number of tankers in the area, and this could be potentially worse for the environment.

With the increased traffic, there are chances of collisions and those could lead to more frequent spills, which would potentially cost billions of dollars to clean up and devastate the coast.
