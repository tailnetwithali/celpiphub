Voice5:

WOMAN: Hello, sir. It’s nice to meet you. I never knew that Canada had a space program.

MAN: I’m surprised that not many people know. As an engineer working in the aerospace industry, my work is directly linked with the goals of Canada’s space advisory board.

WOMAN: What is the Canada Space Advisory Board? Something like NASA?

MAN: No. It’s actually a board made up of senior scientists and government officials, which is run by the Ministry of Innovation Science and Economic Development. It establishes government goals and identifies areas of development that come about through space science. For instance, satellite technology is just one small part of it.

WOMAN: I see. So what has Canada’s involvement in space been like?

MAN: Well, Canada’s involvement in outer space occurred sixty years ago when it became the third country in space with its own satellite, Aloette one.

WOMAN: I never knew this. Fascinating. You said that satellite technology is just a part of the work that has been done. What other things have been done?

MAN: Well, since the launch of Aloette one, Canada has emerged as a key international contributor in all areas of space science and technology. For instance, Canada has contributed not only to unmanned spaceflight but also to human spaceflight and exploration. For example, Canadian scientists contributed heavily towards the development of NASA’s space shuttle robotic arms, as well as the one used by the International Space Station.

WOMAN: Is Canadian society benefiting from all of these advances made by the Canadian space scientists?

MAN: Of course. Every aspect of our lives has had some impact from space research, like I mentioned satellite technology. If we consider communications and data technology, there has been much advancement made in this area all by Canadian research. Without it, communications such as Internet calls, wireless transactions, among others, could never have taken place.

WOMAN: Yes. I can see that. What other improvements have there been as a result of Canadian space research?

MAN: There have been many innovations in space research that have found applications on Earth. For instance, new knowledge and treatments in health science and medicine have taken place such as the neuroarm.

WOMAN: The NeuroArm? What is that?

MAN: The NeuroArm has Canadarm two technology and is based on the robotic arms developed for the space shuttle and ISS. Essentially, the neuroarm is an image guided robotic system that assists neurosurgeons engaged in delicate surgery.

WOMAN: I see. Many argue that there is no direct economic benefit from space research. Your example of the neural arm suggests otherwise.

MAN: There are indeed critics of any space research, but actually space research and development holds a significant role in Canada’s economy and contributes $2,300,000,000 to Canada’s gross domestic product. For instance, around 10,000 Canadians are employed, and over 90% of all Canadian space firms are small to medium sized businesses that make nearly 30% of employment.

Voice7:

MAN: A healthy democracy requires widespread voting and an informed electorate. But by those standards, many, if not most, democracies are ailing. In Canada, less than 60% of the voting age population turned out to vote in the two thousand fifteen federal elections.

Even so, many voters have little idea of the basics of the Canadian system and fewer than a quarter know who their senators are. In a recent symposium on voting rights, political theorists and academic experts pointed out arguments for and against the idea of mandatory voting.

William Galston, a professor at Brookings Institution, Alberta, contends that going to the polls is a duty every responsible being owes to the society to make the political system work better.

However, his colleague, Julia Doris, opposed mandatory voting saying that this approach overlooks crucial ways in which it might actually make the system worse. She further said that on average, those who choose not to vote are even less well informed about politics and public policy than current voters are.

If they are forced to go to the polls, they will worsen the already severe problem of political ignorance. When relatively ignorant voters go to the polls, they aren’t doing the rest of society a favor. They are instead inflicting harm on us by making poor choices and incentivizing politicians to cater to their ignorance.

Political analyst Brennan Walsh admitted that there are some situations where political ignorance can actually be beneficial, and ignorant voters might make better decisions than more knowledgeable ones, though he warned that such cases are unusual.

Most of the time, the most ignorant potential voters can better serve society by staying home on the day of the election than by voting. As Brennan argues, there are many other ways for citizens to contribute to the public good. He advocated that voting is not only or even the best way for people to do their civic duty.

Doctor Marla Pennington is a professor of psychology, and she believes that mandatory voting would be less dangerous if it could be combined with an effective strategy for increasing political knowledge.

She further revealed that doing so through education and other traditional means has turned out to be extremely difficult. Unconventional strategies such as paying voters to become better informed also have serious pitfalls.

Jeffrey McCain, a senior political adviser, is hopeful though. He feels that mandatory voting would not be a complete catastrophe. However, he added that it would make the political system only modestly worse than it already is.

Enforcement involved in mandatory voting is itself harmful, one that at the very least requires a substantial societal benefit to justify.
