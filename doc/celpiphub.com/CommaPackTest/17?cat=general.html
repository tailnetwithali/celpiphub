WOMAN: I want to thank you for your time. I understand that you are a railroad historian, and I’m eager to learn about the transcontinental railroad of Canada.

MAN: You’re quite welcome. While I was serving as the curator of the Canadian Railways Museum, I’d written a number of books on the history of Canadian railroads, and one of them mentioned about it.

WOMAN: Amazing. What can you tell us about Canada’s Continental Railroad? I know prior to 1860, there were a number of railways operating in Canada, but it wasn’t until then when a transcontinental railroad was attempted.

MAN: Actually, that’s not really true. You see, it wasn’t until the eighteen sixties when the first successful attempt at a transcontinental railroad was completed. It took twenty years for the Canadian Pacific Railway to accomplish this. Now prior to 1849, there were only two railways operating in Canada. Both were short lines, serving a predominant area.

WOMAN: What were these railroads?

MAN: The first was the Champlain and Saint Lawrence Railroad, which opened in 1836 in Montreal. It operated seasonally and linked traffic between the Saint Lawrence River Valley and Lake Champlain. Goods would be collected on the Saint Lawrence River and then transported to Lake Champlain and then be carried to other areas by ship.

WOMAN: How many other attempts were made after this?

MAN: Well, there weren’t other attempts to create a transcontinental railroad. But when the guarantee act of 1849 was passed, an increased expansion of railways was seen through all Canadian territories because the government guaranteed profits. This not only allowed for excessive growth in areas that were not profitable, but it also resulted in bankruptcy of many territorial governments.

WOMAN: So going back to what you said earlier, the Canadian Pacific Railroad was the first successful attempt. Why was that so?

MAN: Well, because the territorial governments were bankrupt, the financing for the railroad required a different approach. This was done by allowing financing through private funds and massive land grants in the Canadian Prairies. Moreover, the government was able to provide $25,000,000 and guaranteed a monopoly of all rail traffic to the company that did this. So this led to the formation of the Canadian Pacific Railroad as a private company.

WOMAN: Were there any stipulations that the Canadian Pacific Railroad had to keep?

MAN: Yes. And it was this. The railroad had to be constructed completely under the government’s control.

WOMAN: Wow. When was the railroad completed?

MAN: It was finished in 1885 with the final spikes being driven in several places. The most significant was the final spike in British Columbia, which marked its end at the western portion of the continent. Effectively, the railway linked British Columbia in the West to the Maritime Provinces in the East.

WOMAN: This must have been a very significant achievement.

MAN: Yes. Of course. It allowed for the unification of all Canadian territories. Also, it led to a booming of the Canadian economy for the next thirty years.

WOMAN: I would imagine it also opened up large areas of Canada for settlement.

MAN: Indeed, it did. And it was the construction of the Canadian Pacific Railroad that marked the golden age of the Canadian Railways.

WOMAN: The Manitoba ban provisional government has voiced its decision to ban the use of cell phones in all classrooms. While some may herald this ban, there are many who are voicing concerns that this ban will be both ineffective and impossible to enforce.

The ban is to begin at the start of the next school year according to a press release by education minister Leon Russell. She goes further by stating that this decision is supported by a survey in which 97% of the respondents have endorsed such a ban.

Whilst this may be true, many educationalists and parents argue that the government is moving too quickly to introduce such a ban and cite that there’s not enough evidence to support the scope of such a ban.

Robert Reid of the Manitoba Institute of Education Studies at Winnipeg University believes that one can’t simply ask students to get rid of their cell phones. It’s like telling them not to eat or breathe. Reid feels that the ban is not going to work. Frances Komba is another critic. She’s a former parent association president of Winnipeg College.

She notes that enforcing such a ban will be challenging. According to her, this would lead to the development of a special staff police force to ensure the ban is enforced. In the end, this would waste time, and little work would get done as a result.

There have been some schools and school boards who’ve introduced similar bans throughout the nations recently. However, Manitoba is the only province to do so if the ban takes effect in the fall. According to government sources, it will be up to individual schools and boards to enforce the ban and how to enforce it.

This could include turning in of phones during class times, casual agreements between students and teachers, or other means. Minister Russell said in a press conference that using cell phones distracts students from learning.

The ban is to help students focus on acquiring the necessary skills needed in arithmetic, writing, and reading. Russell’s claim is supported by several studies, including a paper written in 02/2014, which found that student performance can actually increase in a significant manner when cell phones are banned.

However, Reid claims that such findings merely confirm what he suggests as a working assumption. Essentially, it’s an assumption that phones are distracting. He notes that what’s needed is an intensive study to be conducted to determine once and for all the status of cell phone use in the classroom.
