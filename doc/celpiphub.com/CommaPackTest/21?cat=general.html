Voice5:

WOMAN: It’s so nice to meet you, doctor Simpson. With winter coming on, what are some of the dangers that our audience should be aware of?

MAN: Well, our bodies are affected by cold in a variety of ways. As an emergency care physician, I unfortunately get to see all the problems cold weather can bring.

WOMAN: I could imagine. What would you say is perhaps the most dangerous affliction that can be caused by freezing conditions?

MAN: That would have to be frostbite. While both frostbite and hypothermia can be deadly, frostbite can cause more lasting damage should someone be afflicted with it.

WOMAN: Living so far north, I would imagine that frostbite happens quite a lot.

MAN: Actually, it doesn’t. Hypothermia probably happens more often. However, even that is uncommon because we generally are prepared for living in the cold.

WOMAN: Okay. I know hypothermia is the lowering of body temperature. Could you explain what frostbite is?

MAN: Frostbite occurs when the soft tissues of our bodies literally freeze due to overexposure to cold conditions. Typically, it results in the loss of feeling and color in affected areas. For instance, it often affects the nose, ears, cheeks, chin, and fingers because these are the areas that are most often exposed to the air.

WOMAN: Now, can frostbite be easily treated?

MAN: It can be if it’s treated as and when it happens. If not, the victim is likely to suffer permanent damage, including amputation of the affected area. Now there are ways to treat it. First, you have to warm the area. This can be done by soaking the affected area in warm water for twenty to thirty minutes. Now if it’s, say, an earlobe, nose, or cheek, you can use warm wet towels.

WOMAN: Wouldn’t dry heat work better?

MAN: Actually, not. You see, if you use dry heat such as a heating pad, the affected skin is likely to burn easily, and that would cause more injury. Immersion in warm water helps bring up the temperature of the affected area gradually and reduces the risk of physical damage.

WOMAN: Recently, a group of tenants met to discuss issues with the landlords in Edmonton. The packed hall in the community center was filled with frustrated renters recounting stories of landlords’ unresponsiveness or delays in making repairs.

There were concerns over safety in the buildings and the general attitude of who cares held by the building owners. Unfortunately, these problems are not just limited to one building or holdings of a few owners, but epidemic throughout the city.

This is the assessment of Rebecca Johnson of the fair housing program, who is the intake coordinator. According to Johnson, many problems are experienced by low income tenants, most of whom deal with numerous barriers within the housing assistance system.

Often, these tenants are unaware of their rights as tenants, and as a result, they accept their situations as they are. This, in turn, has led to little change for those landlords who cater to these individuals and families.

During this particular meeting of the Fair Housing program, renters were persuaded to approach the Residential Housing Authority for filing repair requests as well as complaints.

However, many people expressed that this process is ineffective, and by filing such paperwork, many fear they could lose their homes. According to Johnson, the Fair Housing program, which is a community based nonprofit organization, was created to assist both landlords and tenants in complying with the rental agreements.

However, Johnson notes that this was not enough because there were many gaps within the housing system which have allowed such problems to exist. She notes that what was needed was a more intensive advocacy on behalf of the tenants.

While many individual landlords have remained relatively silent on the issue, a source within the Edmonton Realty Association has suggested that the possible reason for this indifference on behalf of the landlords is due to the fact that providing rental housing entails a high overhead for the landlords already.

The landlords who cater to low income tenants don’t always have the resources to make repairs as promptly as tenants would like. According to one tenant who was present at the meeting, this sentiment expressed by the source from the ERA is completely unjustified.

She notes that in her building of 200 units, residents pay as much as a thousand dollars per month as rent depending on the size of the apartment. The owners also receive funding from the provincial government in the name of rental assistance.

Johnson sympathizes with the renters and others. However, she states that the problem lies within the system. She notes that there are two underlying issues to the problem. The first is that for the most part, tenants are not using the system.

Secondly, the system is filled with a lot of procedural red tape that makes it difficult for tenants to navigate.
