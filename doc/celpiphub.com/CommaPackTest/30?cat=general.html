Voice5:

MAN: I want to thank you for meeting me. Well, I’m pursuing a master’s in fine arts, and I’m researching on traditional native art. Can you tell me about Canadian native Indian art?

WOMAN: I’d be happy to help, and I can help a lot. I know a lot about native art, more so because I am researching on traditional arts myself.

MAN: Well, what can you tell me about the native art that is kept here at the museum?

WOMAN: Primarily, we house artifacts of the coastal Salish people that make up a number of different tribes living along the Western Coast. Now these people have a very rich culture filled with many diverse objects of art.

MAN: Can you give me some examples?

WOMAN: Yes. For instance, the Salish people were well known for their wood carvings. These included masks, wooden boxes, rattles, and even totem poles. Now the most interesting feature of their art was the use of what are called house posts.

MAN: House posts? I wonder what they are and what’s their significance?

WOMAN: These are large beams on the sides of the longhouse called house posts. These were carved or painted depicting ancestors, family history, or supernatural beings. These were primarily used for two purposes. First, to establish which family lived in the house, and second, to display the family’s spiritual guardians.

MAN: Interesting. So entire families lived in the houses?

WOMAN: Yes. They did. And they had extended families with several generations living in the house, and those houses were often very large.

MAN: Wow. Now you mentioned other wood carvings that Coast Salish people were engaged in.

WOMAN: Yes. The most significant one was their masks. In fact, there is a mask tradition called the mask ceremony, which is an important part of their culture.

MAN: Can you tell me more about it, please?

WOMAN: Sure. Men from certain families were into the Squash Squash society and wore special masks. The masks had bulging eyeballs and horns that resembled animal heads with drooping tongues and crowned large feathers. Such feathers are not found in any other native culture in Canada.

MAN: Interesting. Was there a distinction between the crafts done by men and those done by women?

WOMAN: Yes. For instance, for the Squish Squish masks ceremony, the men would make the masks. Men typically carved house posts, grave monuments, masks, and ritual items, such as rattles, while women crafted woven items, like clothes and blankets.

Voice6:

MAN: Recently, after a five year journey across the nation, Frederican native Roberta Sinclair returned to establish herself as a professional artist and is currently taking New Brunswick by storm.

During her five year trek, Roberta and her husband, Clyde, and their daughter sought to view Canada’s natural beauty. But what really struck her was not so much the nation’s natural beauty, but the degradation that is happening throughout many wilderness areas.

She states that she has always been an artist, but it wasn’t until after returning home that she decided to focus her energies solely on painting. And it was largely the degradation that she witnessed which inspired her to take brush to canvas as a means of expressing and educating people about Canada’s fragile ecosystems.

She also hopes to inspire people through her art to take action to support environmental issues.

She believes that her art is designed to make people question their feelings and attitudes about the environment, such as, is it too late? What can be done? And what is my role? For instance, one painting, which is now hanging in the New Brunswick Provincial Government Building, depicts an eclipse over a diminishing forest.

The forest represents the natural upland forests of the province, which are being diminished due to logging.

While the eclipse has several meanings, such as the forest’s fate is already sealed or time is at hand to make change. Roberta says the different colors represent several concepts.

The blackness of the sky with red light radiating from the eclipsed sun represents danger and a sense of urgency to save the upland forests, whereas the deep greens represent life and environment. While she works in various mediums, she prefers natural pigments including natural dyes and even crushed minerals.

She also paints on wood, particularly birch panels. She believes that using these natural elements help her to connect more deeply with the environment and her subject matter.

Voice7:

MAN: The Ontario government has just announced plans to change the current curriculum for high school students, as well as the class size. These changes are scheduled to take place during the next school year.

However, these changes are prompting an outcry from educators. At the heart of the changes, there is a plan to increase class size from 22 to 28 students. Also, the students would be required to take four mandatory classes online.

According to Erin Roy, president of the local district of the Ontario Secondary School Teachers Federation, such a change in class size is neither manageable nor sustainable.

Many other educators share the same concern. Here in Windsor, this will create additional stress on teachers. Sharon Parsons, president of student government at Windsor High, expressed that teachers are already challenged with current curriculum guidelines and regulations.

She points out that many teachers often have little time for individual help and increasing the class size will make it impossible.

Despite this, some students have expressed otherwise. Ryan Hold Herzog, a senior at the school, does not find it a big concern, particularly in light of the fact that students will now need to take online classes.

According to him, basic courses such as science and mathematics could be taken online and this would even free teachers from spending more time on advanced classes.

Even so, according to the chair of the school board, Marina Klaminski, online learning doesn’t always work well for all students. She mentions that there will be little support in place for students who are having difficulty in grasping subject matter.

She also questions the reason for this big push towards e learning. She further adds that questions regarding the rationale for changing the curriculum are yet to be answered by the government.

This view is shared by John Hansen, Dean of Education at Ontario University. He notes the ambiguity regarding the rationale behind this move by the government. He does note that there is room to enhance computer skills and digital literacy.

But there, he questions if this is a ploy by the government to save money. The Ministry of Education has been contacted, but so far there has been no official response.

However, one insider who spoke on condition of anonymity has stated that the goal was to encourage computer literacy. This person also acknowledged the concerns educators have and has said that the government is planning to hold meetings with educators to address these concerns prior to implementing them next year.
