Voice5:

MAN: Hello, ma’am, and thank you for taking some time to talk. I’m currently researching the culture of the nineteen twenties. Right now, I’m focusing on women and what their life was like during that time.

WOMAN: It’s my pleasure, and this is right up my street as I’m a professor of women’s studies here at the university.

MAN: We all know that, and you are quite famous. So first up, one of the things that stands out during the nineteen twenties, particularly in The United States, was the flapper culture for women. I’m wondering if it ever came to Canada and had any impact.

WOMAN: It actually did come to Canada and quickly swept through Toronto and many of the major cities. It also had a major impact on the urban society here in Canada. One of the aspects of the flapper culture was that it encouraged independence among women. They did things that were, in general, unheard of.

MAN: Could you elaborate, please?

WOMAN: Certainly. For instance, much of the behavior displayed by flappers was never seen prior to the first World War. But after that, women began wearing lipstick in public. They were going to movies and dance halls. They mostly wore short skirts, boots that were left undone, cut their hair short, listened to jazz, and generally partied constantly.

MAN: What was the reason for this?

WOMAN: It was primarily an open showing of their hatred for the old ways. In their own way, they were stating that they were free to do as and what they pleased.

MAN: Alright. But what sort of impact did they make here in Canada?

WOMAN: Well, for Canada, a majority of these women actually pushed for a real change. They began working outside the home and challenging women’s traditional roles. One of the main areas was in jobs. Many of the manufacturing jobs during the time were held by men. But due to World War one, Canada suffered a severe shortage of male workers as it sent many young men off to the war. Women took these jobs, replacing men. Many of the paper and wood mills were being operated by women, especially here in Toronto in which many of the mills became some of the first to do this.

MAN: What other kinds of changes took place?

WOMAN: One other significant change was the fact that these women changed the demographics for many of our large cities. More often than not, they worked and remained unmarried far longer than their rural counterparts, had less children, and helped pave the way for the inclusion of women in Canadian society.

Voice6:

MAN: A Saskatoon woman recently established her taxi company and is being seen by many as a very big move, especially considering the fact that there is little representation of women within this industry.

This was the view of Laura Anderson as she opened Laura’s taxi. She believes that as a woman, she will receive lots of support, especially from other women, particularly because many women have expressed that they would prefer a female driver.

This was especially evident when she witnessed the overwhelming support from her female passengers. According to Laura, her female riders are surprised by seeing her and often express that they feel more comfortable with a woman driver.

Laura goes on and states that this sense of comfort is evident by the fact that women seem to be more relaxed and talk freely with her during their rides, and this is true for women of all ages.

Laura’s cab company seems to be an important move as many women in the past have expressed issues involving safety while riding cabs. For instance, in 02/2015, an advisory board released a report about women and what their needs were. One of the findings was that many young women refused to take cabs alone because they felt unsafe or uncomfortable.

Even sharing rides was felt to be unsafe because there were chances of strangers learning their personal addresses. According to a woman’s rights advocate, safety is one of the foremost issues women are constantly occupied with.

Thus, the idea of female cab drivers comes as a welcome relief. Currently, Laura is the sole driver in her company, but she intends to recruit more women drivers as her business expands.

She is looking forward to this move and suggests that her company is not just a means of earning profit, but a way to safeguard the safety of women in Saskatoon.
