Practice Test A - Reading Part 3: Reading for Information

Time remaining: 10 minutes

Read the following message.

A. Sleepwalking — also known as somnambulism — involves getting up and walking around while in a state of sleep. More common in children than adults, sleepwalking is usually outgrown by the teen years. Isolated incidents of sleepwalking often don’t signal any serious problems or require treatment. However, recurrent sleepwalking may suggest an underlying sleep disorder. Sleepwalking in adults has a higher chance of being confused with or coexisting with other sleep disorders as well as medical conditions and is thought to be genetic. Children who have one or both parents with a history of sleepwalking also have a much higher chance of sleepwalking. It has been found that there are certain genes associated with sleepwalking.

B. Sleepwalking usually happens in the first few hours of sleep during deep sleep. Not all sleepwalkers actually walk. Some sit up or stand in bed or act like they’re awake (but dazed) when, in fact, they’re asleep! But most do get up and move around for a few seconds or for as long as half an hour. Sleepwalkers’ eyes are open, but they don’t see the same way they do when they’re awake. They’ll often think they’re in different rooms of the house or different places altogether. Sleepwalkers tend to go back to bed on their own and they won’t remember what happened in the morning. Sleepwalking may run in families and sometimes happens when a person is sick, has a fever, is not getting enough sleep, or is stressed.

C. Even though seeing someone walking in their sleep can give you what you feel is a mini heart attack, waking a sleepwalker will not cause them to have a heart attack or cause other serious trauma like this medical myth suggests. Sleepwalking itself does not cause any health problems. It can cause problems indirectly mainly due to safety concerns — walking out of the house, jumping out of the window, using lighters and kitchen knives, bumping into sharp objects, etc. There is no consensus on what is the best approach when one encounters a sleepwalking patient. It is not dangerous to wake up a patient from sleepwalking, but experts who discourage it quote it is unsuccessful and leads to patient disorientation. Try to ease them back to bed without making forceful attempts. If unsuccessful, just watch closely to assure their safety and try again after some time has passed.

D. If sleepwalking happens a lot, every night or so, it’s a good idea to see a doctor. But sleepwalking once in a while usually isn’t something to worry about. It’s important, of course, to keep a sleepwalker safe. Precautions should be taken so the person is less likely to fall down, run into something, or walk out the front door while sleepwalking. There’s no cure for sleepwalking, but the doctor can talk to the patient about what’s happening and try to find ways to help them sleep more soundly. For kids who sleepwalk often, doctors may recommend a treatment called scheduled awakening. This means the parent will gently wake them up a little before their usual sleepwalking time, which can help stop sleepwalking. In rare cases, a doctor may prescribe medicine to help someone sleep. Relaxing before bedtime by listening to soft music or having a regular sleep schedule can also help curb this issue. Avoiding alcohol in the evening and going to the bathroom before going to bed can also help.

E. None of the given passages
